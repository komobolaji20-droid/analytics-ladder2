Lesson 1: Getting to Know Excel

Excel is a giant grid of boxes, like graph paper on your computer. Each box is called a cell, and every cell has an address, just like a house has an address on a street. The box in the first column and first row is called A1. The one next to it is B1.

Let's bring back our snack shop from Part 1, and give it a proper home in Excel. Open a blank sheet and type this in, one row per snack, starting in cell A1:

Snack Type Price Units Sold
Chin Chin Snack 200 15
Coke Drink 150 20
Meat Pie Snack 300 8
Fanta Drink 150 12

That's cell A1 through D5. Notice we're using the exact same numbers from Part 1. Every lesson in this part will keep building on this same little table, so type it in now if you're following along.

A few things worth knowing before you go further. When you write a formula and copy it to other cells, Excel normally shifts the reference automatically. This is called a relative reference. If you write =C2*2 in cell E2 and copy it down to E3, Excel automatically changes it to =C3*2, following you down the sheet. Sometimes you want a cell to stay locked in place no matter where you copy the formula, and you do that by adding dollar signs, like $C$2. This is an absolute reference.

You can also give a cell a name instead of remembering its address. Instead of "the total is in D6," you could name that cell TotalUnits and write formulas like =TotalUnits*10 that actually read like plain English.

Now you try it

  1. In cell E2, write a formula that multiplies Price by Units Sold for Chin Chin, then copy that formula down to E3, E4, and E5. What total revenue does each snack show?
  2. Which snack, according to your new column, earned the shop the most money? Does it match the snack with the highest Units Sold, or a different one?
  3. If you copied the formula from E2 down to E5 and it used $C$2 instead of C2, what would go wrong?

The takeaway

Every skill in Excel builds on this one idea: cells have addresses, and formulas use those addresses to do the thinking for you. You'll use this exact snack shop table for the rest of this part, so keep it open.

Lesson 2: Core Formulas and Lookup Functions

Every formula in Excel starts with an equals sign. Beyond simple multiplication, a handful of formulas cover most of what you'll ever need day to day.

SUM adds up a range of numbers. SUMIFS does the same thing, but only for rows matching a condition, so you could total sales just for "Snack" type items and ignore drinks entirely. COUNTIFS counts matching rows instead of summing them. AVERAGEIFS finds an average, again only among matching rows. And IF makes a decision inside a cell: IF(condition, value_if_true, value_if_false).

There's also a family of lookup functions for finding information inside a big table. The modern one is XLOOKUP, written as XLOOKUP(what_to_find, where_to_look, what_to_return).

Let's use these on our shop's table. To find total revenue for just the Snack category, you'd write =SUMIFS(E2:E5, B2:B5, "Snack"), which checks column B for "Snack" and sums the matching revenue in column E. To find one snack's price without scrolling to look for it, =XLOOKUP("Meat Pie", A2:A5, C2:C5) searches column A for "Meat Pie" and returns its price from column C, which is 300.

Now you try it

  1. Write a SUMIFS formula that totals revenue for just the "Drink" category from your table. What number do you get?
  2. Write an AVERAGEIFS formula that finds the average Units Sold for snacks only (Type = "Snack"). How does that compare to the average for drinks?
  3. Use XLOOKUP to find the Units Sold for "Coke" without looking at the table directly. Does the number it returns match what you can see in row 3?

The takeaway

Formulas do the repetitive thinking so you don't have to. The moment you start typing numbers into a calculator by hand instead of using a formula on data that's already in your sheet, you've missed the whole point of Excel.

Lesson 3: Text Functions and Data Cleaning

Real-world data is almost never as neat as our shop's table. Names get extra spaces, text ends up in the wrong case, and the same entry sometimes gets typed twice by accident. Before you analyze anything, you usually clean it first, the same way you'd wash vegetables before cooking.

A few functions handle most cleaning jobs. TRIM removes extra spaces from text. LEFT and RIGHT grab characters from the start or end of a piece of text. TEXTJOIN or CONCATENATE join pieces of text together. And for actual duplicate rows, Excel has a built-in Remove Duplicates tool under the Data tab.

Suppose the person logging our shop's sales got sloppy one day and typed "Meat Pie" with a trailing space, and another day typed " meat pie" in lowercase with a leading space. To Excel, these look like three completely different snacks, even though they're the same item. =TRIM(A2) would strip the extra spaces, but wouldn't fix the capitalization on its own; you'd combine it with UPPER or PROPER to standardize the case as well: =PROPER(TRIM(A2)) turns any messy version into a clean "Meat Pie" every time.

Now you try it

  1. Type " Chin Chin " into a new cell, deliberately including extra spaces at the start and end. Write a TRIM formula that cleans it and confirm the result matches your original A2 exactly.
  2. If your shop's logger accidentally typed "coke" in lowercase on one day and "Coke" with a capital on another, what would happen if you tried to SUMIFS by "Coke" without first cleaning the text?
  3. Write a formula using PROPER and TRIM together that would fix both problems from question 2 in one step.

The takeaway

Messy data leads to wrong answers, even if every formula afterward is technically perfect. Cleaning always comes first, and it's usually the least exciting but most important step in the whole process.

Lesson 4: Data Validation and Conditional Formatting

Have you ever filled out a form online where you had to pick from a dropdown instead of typing your own answer? That's data validation, and it stops the wrong kind of information from being entered in the first place.

Go to the Data tab and choose Data Validation, and you can restrict a cell to only accept a dropdown list of choices, numbers within a range, or dates within a range. This is exactly how the "coke" versus "Coke" problem from Lesson 3 gets prevented at the source, instead of cleaned up after the fact.

Conditional Formatting is a different tool that changes how a cell looks based on its value, without you manually colouring anything yourself.

Let's set up both for our shop. In column A, apply Data Validation with a dropdown list containing exactly "Chin Chin," "Coke," "Meat Pie," and "Fanta," so whoever logs sales can only pick from that exact list, never a slightly different spelling. Then apply Conditional Formatting to your Units Sold column so that any value below 10 turns red automatically. Looking at our table, Meat Pie's 8 units would immediately show up red, drawing attention to it without you having to notice it yourself while scanning the numbers.

Now you try it

  1. Set up a dropdown list in a new column restricted to just the four snack names. Try typing a fifth, made-up name into that column; what happens?
  2. Apply conditional formatting to your Units Sold column so anything under 10 shows red. Which row turns red, and does it match what you'd expect from Part 1's discussion of Meat Pie?
  3. Add a second conditional formatting rule that turns Units Sold green if it's 15 or higher. Which rows qualify?

The takeaway

Good spreadsheets prevent mistakes before they happen with validation, and make important information visible at a glance with conditional formatting. Both save you from manually checking every single cell yourself.

Lesson 5: Pivot Tables

Imagine our shop's table wasn't four rows but four thousand, covering a full year of daily sales instead of one snapshot. Scrolling through and adding things up manually would take forever. A Pivot Table answers a question like "what's total revenue per snack type?" in seconds, and it's one of the most useful tools in all of Excel.

You take your raw data and drag fields into four boxes: Rows, Columns, Values, and Filters. Excel groups and summarizes everything automatically.

Using our shop's table, drag "Type" into Rows and "Units Sold" into Values. Instantly, without writing a single formula, you get a clean summary showing 23 units sold for Snack (15 Chin Chin plus 8 Meat Pie) and 32 for Drink (20 Coke plus 12 Fanta). That's the same SUMIFS math from Lesson 2, done automatically by dragging two fields instead of typing a formula.

Now you try it

  1. Build a Pivot Table from your shop's data with "Type" in Rows and "Price" in Values, set to show the average instead of the sum. What average price does Snack show versus Drink?
  2. Now change the Values field to show a count instead of a sum or average. How many items fall under each Type?
  3. Compare the Pivot Table's Type totals for Units Sold to the SUMIFS formulas you wrote in Lesson 2. Do the numbers match?

The takeaway

Whenever you find yourself asking "what's the total, average, or count of something, grouped by something else," that's a Pivot Table question, and it's often faster to build than writing the equivalent formula by hand.

Lesson 6: Charts and Visualization in Excel

A chart turns a list of numbers into a picture anyone can understand instantly. In Excel, you highlight your data, click Insert, and pick a chart type.

A Bar or Column Chart compares categories against each other, which is exactly what our four snacks call for. A Line Chart shows change over time, useful once you're tracking sales across multiple weeks instead of one day. A Combo Chart mixes two chart types, like bars for actual sales plus a line for a sales target. And a Sparkline is a tiny chart that fits inside a single cell, handy for showing a quick trend next to each row without taking up a whole chart's worth of space.

Select your snack shop's Snack column and Units Sold column together, then insert a simple Column Chart. Instantly, you can see that Coke's bar towers over Meat Pie's, the same comparison that took a sentence to explain back in Part 1's Lesson 5, now visible at a glance.

Now you try it

  1. Build a Column Chart from your Snack and Units Sold columns. Which bar is tallest, and does it match what you calculated as the top seller back in Lesson 1?
  2. Add a second series to your chart showing Price alongside Units Sold. Does the chart still read clearly with two different measures on it, or does it get confusing?
  3. If you had four weeks of data instead of one day, which chart type from this lesson would you switch to, and why?

The takeaway

Charts aren't decoration. They're a translation tool. Always ask yourself which chart type will make a number easiest for someone else to understand instantly, the same question you learned to ask back in Part 1.

Lesson 7: Exploratory Data Analysis and Summary Statistics

Before building any dashboard or drawing any conclusion, you should explore your data first, the same way you'd walk around a new house before deciding where to put your furniture. This is called Exploratory Data Analysis, or EDA.

Aggregated data means summarized, like "total revenue: ₦9,300." Disaggregated data is broken down into detail, like our shop's row-by-row snack list. You'll constantly move between both views. For summary statistics, AVERAGE, MEDIAN, MAX, MIN, and COUNT cover most of what you need, or you can use the Data Analysis ToolPak's Descriptive Statistics option to get all of them at once.

Run these on your shop's Price column: the average price is (200+150+300+150)/4, which is 200. The maximum is 300 for Meat Pie, the minimum is 150 (shared by Coke and Fanta), and the count is 4 items. Already, before building a single chart, you've noticed that Meat Pie is priced 50% above the average, which might be worth keeping in mind when you get to Lesson 8's forecasting.

Now you try it

  1. Run AVERAGE, MAX, MIN, and COUNT on your Units Sold column. What does each one tell you?
  2. Which single number from your EDA check would you flag as unusual or worth a second look, and why?
  3. If a fifth snack was added to the table priced at ₦2,000 by mistake (a typo, meant to be ₦200), which of your four summary statistics would immediately look wrong, and which might hide the mistake?

The takeaway

Never jump straight to charts and conclusions. Always explore your data first. You'll often catch mistakes or surprises before they become embarrassing later, the same lesson from Part 1 applied with real Excel tools this time.

Lesson 8: What-If Analysis, Forecasting, and Dynamic Arrays

What if you want to answer a "what if" question, like "what if I raised Meat Pie's price by ₦50, how much more revenue would that bring in?" Excel has tools built exactly for this.

Goal Seek works backward: instead of you calculating the answer, you tell Excel the answer you want and it figures out what input gets you there. The FORECAST function looks at past data and predicts future values, useful once you have several weeks of sales instead of one day. And dynamic array functions "spill" results automatically into multiple cells. UNIQUE lists only the distinct values in a range, FILTER pulls out rows matching a condition, and SORT arranges a list automatically.

Suppose the shop owner wants to know: "If I want ₦10,000 in total revenue instead of my current ₦9,300, how many more Meat Pies would I need to sell, assuming everything else stays the same?" That's a Goal Seek question: set the target cell to your total revenue formula, the goal to 10000, and the cell to change to Meat Pie's Units Sold. For UNIQUE, =UNIQUE(B2:B5) on your Type column would return just "Snack" and "Drink," even though the column has four rows.

Now you try it

  1. Use Goal Seek to find out how many Meat Pies would need to sell (with everything else unchanged) to reach ₦10,000 total revenue. What number does it give you?
  2. Write a UNIQUE formula on your Snack column. Since all four names are already different, what would you expect the result to look like compared to running UNIQUE on the Type column instead?
  3. Write a FILTER formula that shows only rows where Type equals "Snack." How many rows does it return, and do they match Chin Chin and Meat Pie?

The takeaway

Excel isn't just for looking backward at what already happened. With What-If Analysis and Forecasting, it helps you plan forward too, turning "I wonder what would happen if" into an actual answer.

Lesson 9: Introduction to Power Query

Imagine the shop owner starts getting sales data from three sources every week: one messy spreadsheet from the till, one CSV exported from a delivery app, and one handwritten log typed up later. Cleaning and combining these by hand every single week would eat hours. Power Query is Excel's built-in tool for automating exactly that.

Power Query lets you connect to messy data, clean it step by step, and it remembers every step you took. Next week, when new data comes in, you click Refresh and it repeats the entire cleaning process automatically. This is the beginning of something called ETL: Extract the data, Transform it by cleaning and shaping, then Load it into your worksheet ready to use.

Take our shop's table, but imagine it arrived with the exact messy text problems from Lesson 3: extra spaces, inconsistent capitalization. In Power Query, you'd apply a Trim step and a Capitalize Each Word step once. Every future week's file gets those same two fixes applied automatically the moment you hit Refresh, instead of you manually retyping PROPER and TRIM formulas every single time new data lands.

Now you try it

  1. Open Power Query (Data tab, Get Data) and import your shop's table as a small CSV or Excel file. What do you see in the Applied Steps list after you make even one change, like removing a column?
  2. If you added a Trim step and a Remove Duplicates step in Power Query, in what order would you want them to run, and why does the order matter?
  3. Compare this "clean once, refresh forever" workflow to the manual TRIM and PROPER formulas from Lesson 3. Which approach would you rather maintain if you got a new messy file every single week?

The takeaway

Power Query turns repetitive cleaning work into a one-time setup. This is your first real taste of automation, and it's the exact same thinking you'll use again later in Python.

Lesson 10: Working Efficiently in Excel

The fastest Excel users barely touch their mouse. A handful of keyboard shortcuts, practiced until automatic, save real hours over time.

Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V copy and paste. Ctrl+Z undoes your last action, arguably the most useful shortcut in the entire program. Ctrl plus an arrow key jumps to the edge of your data instantly instead of scrolling. Ctrl+Shift+L toggles filters on and off. Alt+= auto-sums a column in one keystroke. And F4 repeats your last action, or locks a cell reference with dollar signs while you're typing a formula.

On our shop's table, instead of manually selecting E2 through E5 and clicking the AutoSum button in the ribbon, you'd click E6 and press Alt+=, and Excel writes the SUM formula for you instantly. If your table had 500 rows instead of 4, pressing Ctrl+Down Arrow from A1 would jump you straight to the bottom row without scrolling through hundreds of entries by hand.

Now you try it

  1. Click below your Units Sold column and use Alt+= to auto-sum it. Does the total match the 55 units we calculated by hand back in Part 1?
  2. Practice Ctrl+Z by deleting a cell's content on purpose, then undoing it. Did the exact original value come back?
  3. If your shop's table grew from 4 rows to 400, which shortcut from this lesson would save you the most time, and in what specific situation would you use it?

The takeaway

Speed in Excel isn't about typing fast. It's about not wasting time on repetitive clicks. A few shortcuts, practiced until they're second nature, save real hours every single week.
