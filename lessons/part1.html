📊 Lesson 1: Data Basics — What is "Data"?

Let's start simple Have you ever kept score in a game? Or written down your friends' birthdays in a notebook? That's data! Data is simply information we collect and write down — numbers, words, dates, anything that tells us something about the world around us.

Every single day, without even realizing it, you create and use data. When you check the weather before school, that's data. When your mum writes a shopping list with prices, that's data. When your teacher records everyone's test scores, that's data. Data is everywhere — the trick is learning to notice it and understand it.

As a data analyst, your very first job — before touching any software or writing any formula — is simple: look at the information you have, and understand what kind it is. This sounds small, but it's the foundation everything else is built on. Skip this step, and even the fanciest chart or formula will lead you to the wrong conclusion.

Two Simple Types of Data

🔢 Numbers you can count or measure (Quantitative Data) This is any information that comes as a number. Examples: someone's age, how much something costs, how many apples are in a basket, how tall a building is, how many minutes a movie lasts. You can add these up, find an average, or compare them.

🏷️ Words or labels (Qualitative Data) This is information that describes or categorizes something, but isn't a number. Examples: a person's favorite color, the name of a city, "Yes" or "No" answers, a T-shirt size (Small, Medium, Large). You can't "average" these — but you can count how many times each one appears.

Why does the difference matter? Because it changes what you're allowed to do with the data! You can calculate the average age of a class (numbers), but you can't calculate the "average" of everyone's favorite color (labels) — that question doesn't even make sense. Knowing which type you're dealing with stops you from making silly mistakes later.

Let's Try It — A Bigger Example Imagine a small shop that sells snacks. The shop owner wrote this down at the end of the day:

Snack Type Price How Many Sold
Chin Chin Snack ₦200 15
Coke Drink ₦150 20
Meat Pie Snack ₦300 8
Fanta Drink ₦150 12

Let's break this table down column by column: - Snack — this is a label (the name of the item) - Type — this is also a label (it groups items into categories: Snack or Drink) - Price — this is a number (you can add these, compare them, find the most expensive) - How Many Sold — this is also a number (you can add these up to find total items sold: 15+20+8+12 = 55)

See how just this one small table already has both kinds of data mixed together? That's completely normal — most real data looks exactly like this.

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Look around your own house. Pick 5 things (like toys, books, clothes, or fruits). For each one, write down: 1. Its name 2. A number about it (how many you have, or how much it costs, or how old it is) 3. A word/label about it (its color, its type, or its brand)

When you're done, go back through your list and mark each answer with a 🔢 if it's a number, or a 🏷️ if it's a label. Which did you have more of?

✅ Big Idea to Remember Before you can be a great data analyst, you must first know: "Is this a number, or is this a label?" That one simple question opens the door to everything else you'll learn — because every chart, formula, and tool you'll use later on is built to handle one type or the other.

🕵️ Lesson 2: Introduction to Data Analytics — What Does a Data Analyst Do?

Let's start simple Imagine you're a detective. Someone hands you a big pile of clues, and your job is to figure out "what happened" or "what should we do next." You look closely, you connect the dots, and you explain your findings in a way everyone can understand. That is almost exactly what a data analyst does — except the clues are numbers and information, not fingerprints and footprints!

What is Data Analytics, Really? Data analytics means looking at information carefully in order to answer a question or solve a real problem. Companies, schools, hospitals, and even football clubs collect enormous amounts of data every single day. But here's the catch: data on its own doesn't tell you anything. A million rows of numbers sitting in a spreadsheet is useless until someone studies it, makes sense of it, and explains what it means. That someone is you — the data analyst.

The Data Analyst's Real Job, Step by Step 1. Ask a good question — "Why did sales drop last month?" 2. Collect or find the right data — sales records, customer data, dates 3. Clean the data — check for mistakes, missing information, or duplicates 4. Analyze the data — look for patterns, trends, and answers 5. Explain what you found — in a way that helps someone make a decision

Notice that "using Excel" or "writing SQL" isn't even in this list by itself — those are just tools you'll use inside steps 2-4. The real skill is the thinking that connects all five steps together.

A Simple Example A school collects the test scores of 100 students across 5 subjects. On its own, that's just a pile of numbers on a spreadsheet — nobody looks at 500 individual scores and instantly understands anything. But a data analyst can study those numbers and answer real questions like: - Which subject do students struggle with the most, and why might that be? - Are scores getting better or worse compared to last term? - Do students who attend more classes score higher on average? - Are there specific students who need extra help right now?

Each of these answers could genuinely change what the school does next — maybe they hire another Maths teacher, or start an after-school reading club. That's the real power of data analytics: it turns numbers into decisions.

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Think of your favorite football team's last 5 matches. What questions could you ask using that information? Write down 3 questions you could answer if you had the full data (goals scored, home or away, weather, etc.). For each question, guess what the answer might teach the team's coach.

✅ Big Idea to Remember A data analyst's real job isn't just "looking at numbers" — it's asking good questions, then using data to find honest, useful answers that help someone make a better decision.

🤝 Lesson 3: Data Ethics — Doing the Right Thing with Data

Let's start simple Imagine your best friend told you a secret and trusted you to keep it safe. You wouldn't shout it to the whole class, right? Data works the same way. A lot of the data analysts work with is about real people — their names, ages, money, health conditions, or where they live. Data ethics is about treating that kind of information with the same care and respect you'd want someone to treat your own secrets with.

Why This Lesson Matters So Much It's tempting to think ethics is a "boring rules" topic — but it's actually one of the most important things you'll learn. A brilliant analyst who mishandles people's private information can cause real harm: lost trust, financial damage, even danger to someone's safety. Companies can be fined huge amounts of money for handling data carelessly. Being ethical isn't optional — it's part of being a professional.

Key Ideas

🔒 Privacy: Don't share people's personal information without their permission. If you're given access to customer data for a project, that access is only for that project — not for anything else.

✅ Honesty: Never twist numbers, hide inconvenient results, or cherry-pick data to make something look better or worse than it really is. Your job is to tell the truth the data shows, even when it's not the answer someone wanted to hear.

⚖️ Fairness: Make sure your analysis doesn't unfairly treat one group of people worse than another. For example, if you're building a system to approve loans, make sure it isn't accidentally biased against people from a certain area or background.

🗑️ Data minimalism: Only collect and keep the data you actually need. Just because you can collect more information about someone doesn't mean you should.

A Simple Example Imagine you're analyzing customer data for a company, and while going through the spreadsheet, you notice a customer's phone number and home address. Data ethics means you don't go calling them for personal reasons, and you don't share that list with a friend "just to show them." That information was given to the company for a specific purpose only — and you're borrowing it, not owning it.

Another Example A hospital analyst is studying patient recovery times. Even though they can see patients' full names and medical histories, when they present their findings to the hospital board, they use anonymous labels like "Patient A" and "Patient B" instead of real names — protecting people's privacy while still sharing useful insights.

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Imagine you found out a classmate's test score by accident — maybe you saw it on the teacher's desk. Write down: 1. Should you tell other classmates about it? Why or why not? 2. What would you do instead if you genuinely wanted to help that classmate? 3. How is this situation similar to how a data analyst should treat private company data?

✅ Big Idea to Remember Good data analysts are trusted with real information about real people. Being honest, private, and fair with data is just as important as knowing how to analyze it — a skilled analyst who can't be trusted with data isn't truly skilled at all.

📖 Lesson 4: Data Storytelling — Turning Numbers into a Story

Let's start simple Have you ever tried explaining a movie to a friend who hasn't watched it? You don't just list random scenes in no particular order — you tell it like a story, with a beginning, a middle, and an end, so your friend can actually follow along and get excited. Data storytelling is the exact same idea, but with numbers instead of movie scenes.

Why It Matters So Much Here's an uncomfortable truth: most people — even smart, important people like managers and business owners — don't naturally understand raw numbers and tables. Show someone a spreadsheet with 10,000 rows, and their eyes will glaze over. But show that same person a simple, clear story built from that data, and suddenly they understand it in seconds. Your job as a data analyst isn't finished when you find the answer — it's finished when you've explained that answer clearly enough for someone else to act on it.

Two Types of Data Storytelling

🧑‍💻 Technical storytelling: This is for people who already understand data — other analysts, engineers, or IT teams. With this audience, you can use more detailed charts, technical terms (like "standard deviation" or "correlation"), and deeper explanations, because they already speak that language.

🗣️ Non-technical storytelling: This is for people who don't work with data every day — a shop owner, a school principal, a company's sales team. With this audience, you use very simple words, avoid jargon completely, and lean heavily on clear pictures and short, plain sentences.

Building Blocks of a Good Data Story 1. Start with the "so what" — what should the listener actually care about? 2. Use plain language — replace technical terms with everyday words 3. Show, don't just tell — pair your explanation with a simple chart 4. End with a clear takeaway or action — what should happen next?

A Simple Example Instead of saying: "Sales decreased by 12.4% in Q3 due to a drop in the 18–24 demographic segment," You could say: "Fewer young customers bought from us these past 3 months — that's the main reason sales went down. We might want to run a promotion aimed at younger shoppers."

Notice the second version doesn't just simplify the words — it also adds a clear next step. That's what makes a data story genuinely useful instead of just "translated."

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Take this sentence: "Attendance data shows a 30% decline in Monday class attendance over the last 4 weeks." Rewrite it in your own simple words, as if you were explaining it to your grandmother who has never used a computer. Then add one sentence suggesting what the school might do about it.

✅ Big Idea to Remember It doesn't matter how smart or correct your analysis is if nobody understands it. A good data analyst can explain the exact same result in a simple way and a technical way — choosing the right one depending on who's listening.

🎨 Lesson 5: Data Visualization — Turning Numbers into Pictures

Let's start simple Which is easier to understand: a long list of 50 numbers, or one colorful chart showing the same 50 numbers at a glance? Almost everyone would say the chart! That's exactly what data visualization is — turning numbers into pictures (charts and graphs) so people can understand them in seconds instead of minutes.

Why Our Brains Love Pictures Human brains are wired to process images much faster than text or numbers. This is why a chart showing "sales going up" instantly makes sense, while a table full of numbers takes real effort to read and compare. Good visualization isn't about making things look pretty — it's about making information easier and faster to understand.

Common Types of Charts and When to Use Them

📊 Bar Chart: Best for comparing different categories against each other (e.g., sales of 5 different products, or scores across 5 subjects). Each bar's height or length shows its value — longer means bigger.

📈 Line Chart: Best for showing how something changes over time (e.g., temperature over a week, sales over 12 months). The line lets your eye follow the trend — going up, going down, or staying flat.

🥧 Pie Chart: Best for showing parts of a whole (e.g., how a family spends its total monthly income across rent, food, and savings). Use these sparingly — they get confusing with too many slices.

📉 Scatter Plot: Best for showing the relationship between two numbers (e.g., does studying more hours relate to higher test scores?). Each dot represents one data point.

A Simple Example Instead of saying "Monday: 10 customers, Tuesday: 15, Wednesday: 8, Thursday: 20, Friday: 25" in a sentence — you could draw a simple bar chart with one bar for each day. Instantly, anyone looking at it can see Friday was the busiest day and Wednesday was the slowest, without doing any maths in their head.

A Word of Caution Not every chart is a good chart! A confusing chart with too many colors, too many categories, or the wrong chart type can actually make things harder to understand — not easier. Simplicity almost always wins.

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Track how many hours you sleep each night for one week. Then draw (on paper, even!) a simple bar chart with 7 bars — one for each day of the week, with the bar's height showing hours slept. Look at your chart: which day did you sleep the most? Which day the least? Could you tell that quickly just by looking at a list of numbers instead?

✅ Big Idea to Remember A picture really can say a thousand words. Charts help people "see" the answer instantly instead of having to read and calculate it themselves — but only if you pick the right chart for the story you're telling.

🖼️ Lesson 6: Dashboard Design Principles — Making Things Look Good AND Make Sense

Let's start simple Imagine your bedroom. If clothes, books, and toys are scattered everywhere, it takes forever to find anything. But if things are organized — clothes in the closet, books on the shelf, toys in a box — you can find exactly what you need in seconds. A dashboard is like a well-organized room, but built entirely out of data.

What is a Dashboard, Exactly? A dashboard is a single page or screen filled with charts, numbers, and summaries that helps someone understand a whole situation at a glance — just like a car's dashboard shows the driver speed, fuel level, and warning lights all in one place, without them having to look under the hood.

Key Design Rules

🎯 Keep it simple: Don't cram 15 charts into one small screen. A cluttered dashboard is just as confusing as a messy bedroom — pick only the most important information.

🎨 Use colors with purpose: Use one consistent color to mean "good" (often green) and another to mean "bad" or "needs attention" (often red). Don't just use random pretty colors — every color should mean something.

📍 Put the most important information first: Research shows people naturally look at the top-left of a screen first (in cultures that read left-to-right). Put your single most important number or chart there.

📏 Line things up neatly: Charts and numbers should be aligned in a neat grid, not scattered randomly. Neat alignment makes a dashboard feel trustworthy and easy to scan.

🔎 Match the detail to the audience: A dashboard for a busy CEO should show 3-4 big-picture numbers. A dashboard for a data analyst doing deep investigation can show much more detail.

A Simple Example A school dashboard might show: total number of students at the very top (big and bold), a bar chart of average grades by subject just below it, and a line chart of attendance trends at the bottom. Everything a principal needs to know about the school's health, in one glance — no scrolling, no searching, no confusion.

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Draw (on paper) a simple dashboard for your own life. What 3 things would you want to see about yourself every single morning? (Example: hours slept, homework completed, mood today). Sketch a rectangle representing a screen, and decide where you'd place each of the 3 things — remember, the most important one goes top-left!

✅ Big Idea to Remember A good dashboard doesn't just show numbers — it's carefully arranged so someone can understand the whole story in just a few seconds, without needing anyone to explain it to them.

🔢 Lesson 7: Statistics — Making Sense of Numbers

Let's start simple Imagine your whole class took a test, and everyone got different scores. Statistics helps us answer questions like: "What's the average score?" or "Did most people pass or fail?" or "How spread out were the scores?" Simply put, statistics is the study of "what these numbers are really telling us" — turning a messy pile of numbers into a few simple, meaningful facts.

Why Statistics is a Data Analyst's Best Friend You will use basic statistics in almost every single project you ever do. It's the toolkit that lets you summarize thousands of numbers into just a few that actually mean something to a normal human being.

Key Words to Know

➕ Average (Mean): Add up all the numbers, then divide by how many numbers there are. This tells you the "typical" value.

📏 Range: The biggest number minus the smallest number. This tells you how spread out the data is.

🔁 Most Common (Mode): The number or answer that appears the most often in your data.

🎯 Median: If you lined up all your numbers from smallest to biggest, the median is the one right in the middle. This is useful because, unlike the average, it isn't thrown off by one extremely high or low number.

A Simple Example Five friends have these amounts of pocket money: ₦100, ₦200, ₦200, ₦300, ₦700 - Average (Mean): (100+200+200+300+700) ÷ 5 = ₦300 - Most common (Mode): ₦200 (it appears twice — more than any other amount) - Range: 700 − 100 = ₦600 - Median: Line them up: 100, 200, 200, 300, 700 → the middle number is 200

Notice something interesting: the average (₦300) is actually higher than what most friends have! That's because the one friend with ₦700 pulls the average up. This is exactly why analysts often look at both the average AND the median — together, they tell a much more honest story than either one alone.

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Ask 5 friends or family members how many hours of TV or phone they use in a week. Write down their answers, then find: 1. The average 2. The highest number 3. The lowest number 4. The most common answer, if any repeat

✅ Big Idea to Remember Statistics is just a toolbox of simple, powerful tricks to summarize a big pile of numbers into a few clear numbers that tell you the real story — without you having to read through every single value one by one.

🛡️ Lesson 8: Data Governance — The Rules That Keep Data Safe and Correct

Let's start simple Think about a library. Books are organized by category, there are rules about how long you can borrow a book, and someone makes sure books don't go missing or get damaged. Data governance is exactly like being the librarian for a company's data — making sure it's organized, correct, safe, and used the right way by the right people.

Why Companies Take This So Seriously Without governance, data quickly becomes messy: different departments might name the same thing differently, sensitive information might be seen by people who shouldn't see it, and mistakes can go unnoticed for years. Poor data governance leads to poor decisions — because even the best data analyst can't fix bad, disorganized data with a clever formula.

Key Ideas

🔑 Who can access data: Not everyone in a company should be able to see or change every piece of data. A sales intern probably shouldn't have access to the CEO's salary information, for example.

✅ Data quality: Making sure information is accurate, complete, and not full of mistakes or duplicates. This is an ongoing job, not a one-time task.

📋 Data rules and standards: Companies decide clear rules for how data should be named, stored, formatted, and used — so that everyone across the company is speaking the same "data language."

🗂️ Data ownership: Someone is usually responsible for each set of data — making sure it stays accurate and is used properly.

A Simple Example A hospital keeps patient records. Data governance means only doctors and nurses directly involved in a patient's care (not everyone in the building) can see that patient's medical history — and there are strict rules to make sure records aren't accidentally deleted, changed, or leaked.

Another Example A company might have a rule that every date must be written the same way across all their spreadsheets (say, DD/MM/YYYY) — because if one department writes dates one way and another department writes them differently, any analysis combining the two will be full of confusing errors.

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Imagine you're in charge of your family's shared photo album on a phone or computer. Write 3 rules you'd make about: 1. Who can add new photos 2. Who can delete photos 3. How photos should be labeled or organized (by date? by event?)

✅ Big Idea to Remember Good data isn't just about having lots of information — it's about keeping that information organized, safe, correct, and used the right way by the right people, for as long as it exists.

🏭 Lesson 9: Data for Various Industries — Every Job Uses Data!

Let's start simple You might think data is only useful for banks or tech companies — but that's simply not true! Almost every kind of job, in every kind of industry, uses data in its own way to make better decisions.

Examples Across Different Industries

🏥 Healthcare: Tracking patient recovery rates, hospital bed availability, how long patients wait to see a doctor, which treatments work best.

🛒 Retail: Which products sell the most, the best time of year for sales, which items are frequently bought together, customer shopping habits.

⚽ Sports: Tracking a player's goals, speed, distance covered, and performance over a season, to decide who should start the next match.

🏫 Education: Tracking student grades, attendance, and how different teaching methods affect learning outcomes.

🌾 Agriculture: Tracking rainfall, soil quality, and crop harvest amounts to decide when and what to plant.

🎬 Entertainment: Streaming services track which shows people watch, when they stop watching, and use that to decide what new shows to make.

A Simple Example A farmer who keeps a small notebook noting rainfall each month and how many bags of maize they harvested is already doing data analysis — even without a single computer. If they notice "every year with more rain in April, I get a bigger harvest," they've just discovered a pattern using data — the exact same skill professional analysts use, just at a smaller scale.

Why This Matters for You This means that no matter what career path excites you — medicine, sports, fashion, farming, music — learning data skills will make you better at it. You don't have to choose between "data" and "your passion." Data skills make almost any passion stronger.

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Pick a job you're interested in (doctor, footballer, chef, musician, fashion designer, etc.). Write down 2 things that job could track with data, and explain how having that information would help them do their job even better.

✅ Big Idea to Remember Data skills aren't just for "tech people" working at big companies. No matter what career you eventually choose, understanding data will make you sharper, more useful, and more successful at it.

⚠️ Lesson 10: Common Mistakes Beginners Should Avoid

Let's start simple Everyone makes mistakes when learning something new — that's completely normal, and even the best data analysts in the world made plenty of mistakes when they were starting out! But knowing the common ones ahead of time can save you a lot of frustration and wasted time.

Common Beginner Mistakes

  1. 🏃 Rushing through the basics: Jumping straight to advanced tools like Power BI or Python without truly understanding the "why" behind data basics and statistics. This always catches up with you later.

  2. 🙈 Not checking your data for errors: Working with messy, incomplete, or duplicated data leads to wrong answers — even if every formula you use afterward is technically correct. "Garbage in, garbage out," as analysts like to say.

  3. 📚 Collecting certificates but no real practice: Watching video after video and collecting certificate after certificate, without ever actually building a real project with real (or realistic) data.

  4. 🤐 Not asking questions: Staying silently stuck on a problem for hours instead of asking for help in a community, from a mentor, or even searching online.

  5. 🙅 Comparing yourself to others too much: Feeling discouraged because someone else in your learning group seems to be moving faster. Everyone learns at their own pace, and skipping ahead just to "catch up" often backfires.

  6. 🔍 Skipping the "so what" step: Finding an interesting pattern in the data but never explaining why it actually matters or what should be done about it.

A Simple Example A beginner analyzes a company's sales data but forgets to check for duplicate entries in the spreadsheet — maybe the same sale got recorded twice by accident. Every formula they use afterward "looks" correct, but their final numbers are wrong from the very start, because the mistake happened before the analysis even began.

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Think of a time you rushed through something — homework, a game, a chore, a test — and made a mistake because of it. Write down: 1. What happened 2. What you learned from it 3. What you'd do differently next time

✅ Big Idea to Remember Mistakes are simply part of learning — nobody skips this step. The real goal isn't to avoid every single mistake; it's to slow down, double-check your work, ask for help when you're stuck, and keep practicing consistently.

💻 Lesson 11: Introduction to Version Control & GitHub

Let's start simple Imagine you're writing a story for school, and you save different versions as you go: "Story Draft 1," "Story Draft 2," "Story FINAL," "Story FINAL FINAL." It gets messy fast, right? Version control is a much smarter, more organized way of doing exactly that for your work — especially data projects — so you never lose your progress, and you can always go back to an earlier version if something breaks.

What is GitHub? GitHub is a website where people save, organize, and share their projects with the world. For a data analyst, it's like your online notebook and portfolio combined — a public place where anyone, including a recruiter scrolling through hundreds of applications, can see the actual projects you've built with your own hands.

Key Ideas to Know

📁 Repository ("repo"): A folder that holds everything related to one project — your files, your notes, your data.

💾 Commit: Saving a snapshot of your work along with a short note describing what changed (e.g., "Added sales chart" or "Fixed a formula error").

☁️ Push: Uploading your saved work from your computer up to GitHub, so it becomes visible online for others to see.

📝 README: A simple text file at the front of your project that explains, in plain words, what the project is and why you built it — this is often the very first thing a recruiter reads.

A Simple Example You build a small Excel dashboard analyzing a shop's sales. Instead of keeping it only saved on your own laptop where nobody else will ever see it, you upload it to GitHub with a short README description: "My first sales dashboard project — I explored which products sold best and built a simple visual summary." Now it's visible to the whole world, forever, with your name attached to it.

🧠 Now You Try (Exercise) Write a 2-3 sentence description of a project you'd like to build one day (it doesn't have to be data-related — it could be an app, a game, or even a piece of art). Pretend this is the README description you'd put on GitHub for a stranger to understand exactly what your project does and why you made it.

✅ Big Idea to Remember Learning tools like Excel and SQL makes you skilled. But GitHub is what makes your skills visible to the world. As we said earlier in this course: a project nobody can see doesn't exist to a recruiter — GitHub is how you make sure your work is seen.

📂 Data Management (Overview)

Let's start simple Data management ties everything you've learned in Part 1 together into one big picture. It's the ongoing, never-really-finished practice of collecting, storing, organizing, protecting, and properly using data — from the very moment it's created, all the way until it's no longer needed.

Think of it as combining every single lesson you just completed: - Knowing your data (Lesson 1) — understanding what type of information you're working with - Analyzing it with good questions (Lesson 2) — using data to solve real problems - Handling it ethically (Lesson 3) — protecting the people behind the numbers - Explaining it well (Lessons 4–6) — through storytelling, visuals, and clean dashboards - Understanding it with statistics (Lesson 7) — summarizing numbers into meaningful facts - Protecting and organizing it (Lesson 8) — through governance and clear rules - Applying it to real industries (Lesson 9) — connecting data skills to real careers - Avoiding common mistakes (Lesson 10) — learning from beginner pitfalls before you make them - Showing your work to the world (Lesson 11) — through GitHub and version control

Every single one of these pieces works together. A brilliant analysis, explained badly, helps nobody. A beautiful dashboard built on messy, unethical data is actually dangerous. Data management is the mindset that makes sure all your individual skills add up to real, trustworthy, useful work.

✅ Big Idea to Remember Data management isn't a single skill you learn once and finish — it's the ongoing mindset that ties every other skill in this course together. Now that you've completed Part 1, you're ready to start learning your very first real tool: Excel!
