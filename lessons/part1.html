Lesson 1: What is "Data," Really?

Have you ever kept score in a game? Or written down a friend's birthday in a notebook? That's data. It's simply information we collect and write down: numbers, words, dates, anything that tells us something about the world around us.

You probably create and use data every day without noticing. Checking the weather before school, writing a shopping list with prices, a teacher recording test scores. All of it is data. The trick is learning to notice it and understand what you're looking at.

As a data analyst, your very first job, before you touch any software or write a single formula, is to look at the information in front of you and understand what kind it is. That sounds small. It isn't. Skip this step and even the fanciest chart or formula will still lead you to the wrong conclusion.

Two kinds of data

Most of what you'll work with falls into one of two buckets: numbers you can count or measure, and words or labels that describe something.

Let's make this real. Imagine a small shop that sells snacks. At the end of the day, the owner writes this down:

Snack Type Price How Many Sold
Chin Chin Snack ₦200 15
Coke Drink ₦150 20
Meat Pie Snack ₦300 8
Fanta Drink ₦150 12

Look at the Snack column. "Chin Chin," "Coke," "Meat Pie" are all labels. So is the Type column: "Snack" or "Drink" describes what each item is, not how much of it there is. You can't average a column of labels. Asking "what's the average snack name" doesn't mean anything.

Now look at Price and How Many Sold. These are numbers. You can add them up: 15+20+8+12 = 55 items sold in total. You can compare them: Meat Pie is the most expensive item at ₦300. You can find the average price across all four items.

That's the whole difference. Numbers let you calculate. Labels let you group and count. Knowing which one you're looking at, before you do anything else, decides which formulas and charts will even make sense later.

The same two buckets show up everywhere. A school's attendance sheet has student names (labels) and days present (numbers). A hospital's patient log has diagnoses (labels) and recovery time in days (numbers). Once you can spot the difference in a snack shop's notebook, you can spot it anywhere.

Now you try it

Look back at the snack shop table above and answer these:

  1. Which two columns are labels, and which two are numbers?
  2. Add up the "How Many Sold" column. How many total items did the shop sell that day?
  3. Which snack made the shop the most money? (Multiply price by how many were sold, for each row, then compare.)
  4. If a friend asked "what's the average snack?", why can't that question actually be answered using this table?
  5. Which item sold the fewest units, and what price was it sold at?

The takeaway

Before you can be a good data analyst, you need to be able to answer one question about anything you're looking at: is this a number, or is this a label? That single question decides which tools and techniques apply, and it's the first thing you should ask about any dataset, in this course or in a real job.

Lesson 2: What Does a Data Analyst Actually Do?

Imagine you're a detective. Someone hands you a pile of clues, and your job is to figure out what happened and what should happen next. You look closely, connect the dots, and explain your findings so everyone can understand them. That's basically what a data analyst does, except the clues are numbers instead of fingerprints.

Companies, schools, and hospitals collect huge amounts of data every day. On its own, that data doesn't tell anyone anything. A spreadsheet with a million rows is useless until someone studies it and explains what it means. That someone is you.

The real job breaks down into five steps: ask a good question, collect or find the right data, clean it up, look for patterns, and explain what you found in a way that helps someone decide what to do next. Notice that "using Excel" isn't even on this list. Excel is just a tool you use inside steps two through four. The actual skill is the thinking that connects all five steps.

Let's go back to our snack shop from Lesson 1. Say the owner notices sales have been dropping and asks you to figure out why. You'd start with a question: has one type of snack stopped selling, or has everything slowed down equally? Then you'd pull a few weeks of the same sales record we saw before, not just one day. You'd check it for mistakes: did anyone forget to log a sale? Then you'd look for the pattern: maybe drinks are still selling fine but snacks have dropped. And finally you'd explain it plainly: "Snack sales fell 30% over three weeks, mostly Meat Pie. Might be worth checking if the price went up or a competitor opened nearby."

That last sentence is the whole point of the job. A pile of numbers turned into one clear reason and one clear next step.

Now you try it

Using the same snack shop data from Lesson 1:

  1. If the owner asked "should I stock more Coke or more Fanta next week?", what would you look at in the table to answer that?
  2. Meat Pie sold only 8 units, the lowest of the four. Write down two different possible reasons why, based only on what you can see in the table.
  3. What additional piece of information (not currently in the table) would help you tell which of your two reasons is actually true?

The takeaway

A data analyst's job isn't "look at numbers." It's ask a good question, then use data to find an honest, useful answer that helps someone make a better decision.

Lesson 3: Doing the Right Thing with Data

Imagine your best friend told you a secret and trusted you to keep it. You wouldn't repeat it to the whole class. Data works the same way. A lot of what analysts work with is about real people: their names, ages, money, health, or where they live. Data ethics means treating that information with the same care you'd want someone to treat your own secrets with.

This isn't a "boring rules" topic. An analyst who mishandles private information can cause real harm: lost trust, financial damage, sometimes danger to someone's safety. Companies get fined heavily for handling data carelessly. Being ethical is part of being professional, not an optional extra.

A few ideas matter most here. Privacy means not sharing someone's personal information without permission, even if you technically have access to it. Honesty means never twisting numbers or hiding inconvenient results to make something look better than it is. Fairness means checking that your analysis doesn't unfairly treat one group worse than another, say, a loan approval system that's accidentally biased against people from a certain area. And data minimalism means only collecting what you actually need, not everything you could possibly grab.

Back to our snack shop: imagine the owner asks you to analyze customer loyalty using their phone numbers, collected when people order for delivery. Ethics means those numbers stay in that spreadsheet for that purpose. You don't call a customer to ask them why they stopped buying Meat Pie, and you don't hand the list to a friend who's opening a competing shop nearby.

Now you try it

  1. In the loyalty analysis example above, what's one legitimate way to use the phone number data, and one way that would cross the line?
  2. Imagine you accidentally saw a classmate's test score on a teacher's desk. Should you tell other classmates? Why or why not?
  3. How is your answer to question 2 similar to how an analyst should treat a customer's private data?

The takeaway

Good analysts are trusted with real information about real people. Being honest, private, and fair with data is just as important as knowing how to analyze it. A skilled analyst who can't be trusted with data isn't actually skilled at the job.

Lesson 4: Turning Numbers into a Story

Have you ever explained a movie to a friend who hasn't seen it? You don't list random scenes out of order. You tell it like a story, with a beginning, middle, and end, so they can follow along. Data storytelling works the same way, except with numbers instead of movie scenes.

Here's an uncomfortable truth: most people, even smart, important people like managers and business owners, don't naturally understand raw numbers and tables. Show someone a spreadsheet with 10,000 rows and their eyes glaze over. Show that same person one clear sentence built from the data, and they get it instantly. Your job isn't finished when you find the answer. It's finished when you've explained it clearly enough that someone can act on it.

There are two audiences to think about. Technical storytelling is for people who already understand data, other analysts or engineers, where you can use detailed charts and terms like "correlation" freely. Non-technical storytelling is for people who don't work with data daily, like our snack shop owner, where you need plain words and no jargon at all.

Let's use the sales drop from Lesson 2. A technical version might say: "Snack category revenue declined 30% week-over-week, concentrated in the Meat Pie SKU." A non-technical version, aimed at the shop owner, would say: "Meat Pie sales have dropped by about a third over the last three weeks. Worth checking if the price changed or a new competitor opened nearby." Same finding, same honesty, completely different words, because the two versions are built for different people.

Now you try it

  1. Rewrite this in plain words, as if explaining it to someone who has never seen a spreadsheet: "Drink sales at the shop remained flat, while snack sales declined 30% over three weeks."
  2. Add one sentence to your rewrite suggesting what the shop owner might do next.
  3. Now write a technical version of the same finding, using at least one data term like "trend," "category," or "week-over-week."

The takeaway

It doesn't matter how correct your analysis is if nobody understands it. A good analyst can explain the same result simply and technically, choosing the right version depending on who's listening.

Lesson 5: Turning Numbers into Pictures

Which is easier to understand: a long list of fifty numbers, or one chart showing the same fifty numbers at a glance? Almost everyone picks the chart. That's data visualization: turning numbers into pictures so people understand them in seconds instead of minutes.

Human brains process images far faster than text. A chart showing "sales going up" makes sense instantly, while a table of numbers takes real effort to read and compare. Good visualization isn't about making things pretty. It's about making information faster to understand.

A few chart types come up constantly. A bar chart is best for comparing categories against each other, like our snack shop's four items. A line chart shows change over time, like sales across several weeks. A pie chart shows parts of a whole, and works best with very few slices. A scatter plot shows the relationship between two numbers, like hours studied against test scores.

Take the shop's sales data from Lesson 1. Written as a sentence, "Chin Chin sold 15, Coke sold 20, Meat Pie sold 8, Fanta sold 12" takes a moment to compare. Drawn as a simple bar chart, with one bar per snack, anyone can see instantly that Coke was the biggest seller and Meat Pie the smallest, without doing any mental math at all.

Not every chart is a good chart, though. Too many colours, too many categories, or the wrong chart type can make something harder to understand instead of easier. Simplicity almost always wins.

Now you try it

  1. Using the four items from the snack shop table, which chart type would you use to compare how many units each snack sold? Why that one?
  2. If the owner wanted to see how Coke's sales changed over the last 8 weeks, which chart type would fit better than the one from question 1? Why?
  3. Sketch (on paper) a simple bar chart of the snack shop's "How Many Sold" column, with one bar per item.

The takeaway

A picture can say a thousand words. Charts help people see the answer instantly instead of calculating it themselves, but only if you pick the right chart for the story you're telling.

Lesson 6: Making Dashboards Look Good and Make Sense

Imagine your bedroom. If clothes, books, and toys are scattered everywhere, it takes forever to find anything. Organized, with clothes in the closet and books on a shelf, you can find what you need in seconds. A dashboard is a well-organized room built entirely out of data: a single screen filled with charts and numbers that helps someone understand a whole situation at a glance, the same way a car dashboard shows speed and fuel without you looking under the hood.

A few design rules make the difference. Keep it simple: don't cram fifteen charts onto one small screen. Use colour with purpose: one consistent colour for "good," another for "needs attention," not random pretty colours. Put the most important number where people look first, usually the top-left of the screen. Line things up neatly in a grid rather than scattering them. And match the level of detail to who's looking: a busy owner needs three or four big numbers, not a full spreadsheet.

Let's build one for our snack shop. At the top, big and bold: total items sold today, 55. Just below it, a bar chart comparing all four snacks side by side, the same one you sketched in Lesson 5. At the bottom, a small line showing sales over the past few weeks, so the owner can spot the Meat Pie decline from Lesson 2 without anyone explaining it to them. Three things, arranged by importance, and the owner understands their whole day in five seconds.

Now you try it

  1. Using the snack shop's numbers, what single number would you put in the top-left corner of a dashboard, and why that one over any other?
  2. Where would the bar chart from Lesson 5 go relative to that number, and why?
  3. If the owner only had ten seconds to look at this dashboard each morning, which of the three elements described above could you cut, and which absolutely has to stay?

The takeaway

A good dashboard doesn't just show numbers. It's arranged so someone understands the whole story in a few seconds, without needing anyone to explain it to them.

Lesson 7: Making Sense of Numbers with Statistics

Imagine your whole class took a test and everyone got different scores. Statistics answers questions like "what's the average?" or "how spread out were the scores?" It's the study of what a pile of numbers is really telling you, turned into a few simple, meaningful facts.

A handful of terms cover most of what you'll need. The average, or mean, is all the numbers added up and divided by how many there are. The range is the biggest number minus the smallest. The mode is whichever value shows up most often. The median is the value right in the middle when everything is lined up smallest to biggest, and it's useful because, unlike the average, one extreme number can't drag it around.

Let's use real numbers this time: five friends' pocket money, ₦100, ₦200, ₦200, ₦300, ₦700. The average is (100+200+200+300+700) divided by 5, which is ₦300. The mode is ₦200, since it appears twice. The range is 700 minus 100, or ₦600. The median, lining everything up, is 200, the middle value.

Notice something: the average, ₦300, is actually higher than what four out of five friends actually have. That's because the one friend with ₦700 pulls the average upward. This is exactly why analysts look at both the average and the median together. Used alone, the average can quietly mislead you.

Now you try it

  1. Using the same five amounts, ₦100, ₦200, ₦200, ₦300, ₦700, what would the average become if the ₦700 friend actually had ₦1,200 instead? Recalculate it.
  2. Does the median change if that one friend's amount changes from ₦700 to ₦1,200? Why or why not?
  3. Based on questions 1 and 2, which of the two, average or median, is more affected by one unusually large number?

The takeaway

Statistics is a toolbox of simple, powerful tricks for summarizing a big pile of numbers into a few numbers that tell you the real story, without reading through every single value by hand.

Lesson 8: The Rules That Keep Data Safe and Correct

Think about a library. Books are organized by category, there are rules about how long you can borrow one, and someone makes sure books don't go missing. Data governance is being the librarian for a company's data: making sure it's organized, correct, safe, and used properly by the right people.

Without governance, data gets messy fast. Different departments name the same thing differently, sensitive information ends up visible to people who shouldn't see it, and mistakes go unnoticed for years. Poor governance leads to poor decisions, because even the best analyst can't fix disorganized, untrustworthy data with a clever formula.

A few pieces matter most. Access control means not everyone should be able to see or change every piece of data; a sales intern probably shouldn't see the owner's bank balance. Data quality means the information stays accurate and complete, which is an ongoing job, not a one-time fix. Standards mean everyone names and formats things the same way, so combining data from two sources doesn't turn into a mess. And ownership means someone is responsible for keeping each set of data accurate.

Back to the shop: imagine the owner now has two people logging sales, one on paper in the morning and one on a phone app in the evening. Without a rule that both use the same format, the same snack might get logged as "Chin Chin" in one and "chinchin" in the other, and any analysis combining the two will quietly miscount it as two different items.

Now you try it

  1. In the two-logger example above, write one simple rule that would prevent the "Chin Chin" versus "chinchin" mismatch.
  2. Who in the shop should be allowed to see the full daily sales numbers, and who probably shouldn't need to? Explain your reasoning.
  3. If you were the owner, how often would you check that both loggers are still following the naming rule you wrote in question 1: daily, weekly, or monthly? Why that choice?

The takeaway

Good data isn't just about having a lot of information. It's about keeping it organized, safe, correct, and used the right way by the right people, for as long as it exists.

Lesson 9: Every Job Uses Data

You might think data is only useful for banks or tech companies, but that's not true. Almost every kind of job uses data in its own way to make better decisions.

A hospital tracks patient recovery rates and how long people wait to see a doctor. A retailer tracks which products sell most and what's often bought together, exactly like our snack shop. A football club tracks a player's goals and distance covered across a season. A school tracks grades and attendance. A farmer tracks rainfall and harvest size. A streaming service tracks which shows people watch and when they stop watching.

Actually, look back at our snack shop one more time. Every lesson in this part used the exact same small dataset: four snacks, their prices, and how many sold. That single shop touches retail, small business finance, and even food service all at once. The tools change depending on the industry, but the underlying skill, reading a table and asking good questions of it, is identical everywhere.

Now you try it

  1. Pick a career you're interested in that hasn't been mentioned yet. Write down two things that job could track using a table similar in structure to our snack shop's (an item, a number, and a label).
  2. For the career you picked, what decision could someone make better if they had that data, the same way our shop owner could decide whether to restock Meat Pie?

The takeaway

Data skills aren't just for people working in tech. Whatever career you choose, understanding how to read and question a simple table like our snack shop's will make you sharper at it.

Lesson 10: Mistakes Worth Avoiding Early

Everyone makes mistakes learning something new. Even experienced analysts made plenty when they were starting out. Knowing the common ones ahead of time saves you real frustration.

Rushing through the basics to jump straight to advanced tools always catches up with you later. Skipping the check for errors in your data, duplicates or missing values, leads to wrong answers even when every formula afterward is correct. Collecting certificates without ever building a real project leaves you with no proof you can actually do the work. Staying stuck silently instead of asking for help wastes time that a single question could save. Comparing your pace to someone else's in your learning group usually backfires. And finding an interesting pattern in the data but never explaining why it matters, skipping the "so what," leaves your work half-finished.

Here's how this shows up with our snack shop. Imagine whoever logs sales accidentally enters Coke's sale twice on a busy day: once at 20 units, then again by mistake at another 5. Every formula you run afterward, the average, the total, the bar chart, will be technically correct and completely wrong at the same time, because the mistake happened before any analysis began.

Now you try it

  1. In the double-entry example above, what number would the "How Many Sold" total show for Coke if the mistake wasn't caught, and what should it actually be?
  2. What's one simple check you could run on the sales table each day to catch a mistake like this before it affects any analysis?
  3. Think of a time you rushed through homework or a task and made a similar kind of error. What would "checking your work first" have looked like in that situation?

The takeaway

Mistakes are part of learning. The goal isn't to avoid every one of them; it's to slow down, check your work, ask for help when stuck, and keep practicing.

Lesson 11: Making Your Work Visible with GitHub

Imagine writing a school story and saving different versions as you go: Draft 1, Draft 2, Final, Final Final. It gets messy fast. Version control is a far more organized way of doing exactly that for your work, especially data projects, so you never lose progress and can always go back if something breaks.

GitHub is a website where people save, organize, and share their projects with the world. For a data analyst, it works like an online notebook and portfolio combined, a public place where a recruiter scrolling through applications can see the actual projects you've built.

A few terms are worth knowing. A repository, or repo, is a folder holding everything related to one project. A commit is a saved snapshot of your work with a short note describing what changed. A push uploads your saved work to GitHub so it becomes visible online. A README is a short file at the front of your project explaining, in plain words, what it does and why you built it, often the first thing a recruiter actually reads.

Say you build a small dashboard analyzing our snack shop's sales, the same data we've used throughout this part. Instead of leaving it saved only on your laptop where nobody will ever see it, you upload it to GitHub with a short README: "A sales dashboard analyzing four weeks of a small snack shop's data. Found that Meat Pie sales dropped 30% over three weeks and recommended checking pricing." Now it's visible to anyone, forever, with your name attached.

Now you try it

  1. Write a two or three sentence README description for the snack shop dashboard project described above, as if you were about to publish it on GitHub today.
  2. What's one detail from Lessons 1 through 10 (a specific number, a specific finding) that you'd want to mention in that README to make it feel like a real, specific project rather than a generic one?

The takeaway

Learning tools like Excel and SQL makes you skilled. GitHub is what makes that skill visible to the world. A project nobody can see doesn't exist to a recruiter, and GitHub is how you make sure your work is seen.

Data Management: Bringing It Together

Data management ties everything in this part together. It's the ongoing practice of collecting, storing, organizing, protecting, and properly using data, from the moment it's created until it's no longer needed.

Every lesson you just went through touched a piece of it, and every one of them used the same small snack shop as a thread running through the whole part. You learned to tell a number from a label. You learned to ask good questions of that data. You learned to handle it ethically. You learned to explain it, visualize it, and put it on a dashboard. You learned to summarize it with statistics, keep it organized with governance, connect it to real industries, avoid common mistakes, and finally, make your work on it visible to the world.

None of these pieces work well alone. A brilliant analysis explained badly helps nobody. A beautiful dashboard built on messy, unethical data is actually dangerous. Data management is the mindset that makes sure all of it adds up to something real and trustworthy.

The takeaway

You're not learning eleven separate skills. You're learning one connected way of thinking, and you've now practiced all of it on a single, small dataset you understand completely. That's exactly the mindset you'll carry into the next tool: Excel.
